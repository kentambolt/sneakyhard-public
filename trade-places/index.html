Trade Places

Get the colour groups past each other. The arrows decide how.

How to play

Get the colour groups past each other so they trade sides. A piece only moves in the directions of its arrows: one step into an empty space, or a hop over exactly one piece of any colour into an empty space. No diagonal moves.

Fill every coloured target zone with pieces of the same colour. A connected zone has one outer outline; the faint inner lines are movement cells. Colours with no target zone may finish anywhere. When no legal moves remain, pieces outside their target colour are outlined; undo to try another order.

How the levels were made

The first levels are hand-built shapes whose shortest solutions are proven by an exact solver. Other boards come from a search program that keeps inventing small boards and scores how hard they are for people, not just for computers. Every candidate is solved exactly, so the shortest solution is known. Then seven human-like players try it: one reduces potential future encounters, one gets as many pieces home as possible, one prefers hops, and so on. The level is only as hard as the best of them finds it.

The score also counts how often a random player would win, how many moves lead into positions you can never win from, and how many sensible-looking moves there are at each step. A simplicity penalty keeps boards small: every extra cell, arrow, colour or empty space costs points.

What makes it hard

To keep the levels varied, the pack mixes families instead of just making boards bigger, and the search looks for the hardest board inside each family:

  • lines in one dimension;
  • crosses and overlapping squares, including a board whose shortest solution is 46 moves;
  • compact boards with only 9 to 12 cells;
  • four colours where each has a single, different arrow.

Playtests shaped the rules too. An invisible target once rejected a position that clearly looked complete. Every target is now visible, with one continuous outline per connected zone. Twelve boards have one final arrangement; three use only the target colour needed to show that the groups have passed. Every shortest route has been re-proved for these targets. Target shape also counts toward simplicity: connected rectangles beat scattered islands. One four-colour board gained one cell to form four rectangles; its shortest solution rose from 33 to 34 moves. Completing its 5×5 outline later changed no moves: the three extra squares are unreachable with these arrows. Another board gained a green 2×2 target and still needs 35 moves. A later review removed an easier near-duplicate whose starting pieces differed by just one move, keeping the pack varied.

Tips

  • Look for the piece that can only move one way. It often decides the order.
  • Empty spaces are precious. Count where they need to be before you move.
  • A hop can move a piece two cells at once, also over a piece of another colour.

Made by search, tested by people

Every SneakyHard level is chosen by a search program that models how people solve puzzles: few pieces, simple rules and as much challenge as they can hold. We build these solvers together with AI and refine them through playtests.
